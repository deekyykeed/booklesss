# Discipline profiles

Loaded by **C-11**, before writing the first section of a course this skill has
not written before.

`RULES.md` is the house style for every Booklesss course. Most of it is about
writing and holds anywhere: **W-1** through **W-17**, all of **S**, **C-1**,
**C-2**, **C-5** through **C-8**, **C-10**. Those are not repeated here and are
not up for negotiation by discipline.

What this file settles is the handful of rules whose *meaning* changes with the
subject, and it exists because every course built so far has been finance.
A rule written from finance and applied to a law course is not slightly off; it
is confidently wrong, and it will look correct while being so.

## The one question that picks the profile

**What does the exam ask the reader to produce?**

Not "what is the subject" — subjects blur, and a management course can examine
like a law paper. What the reader has to *make* on the day is what decides how
a step is built, and it is usually answerable in one line off a past paper.

| If the paper asks them to… | Profile |
|---|---|
| Compute a number and show the working | **Quantitative** |
| Apply a rule or test to a set of facts and reach a defensible conclusion | **Rule-application** |
| Argue a position and support it | **Discursive** |
| Carry out a procedure correctly, in order | **Procedural** |

A course can be two of these in different lessons, and most are. **Pick per
lesson, not per course**, and say which in each step's header comment.

Where a paper is genuinely mixed inside one step, the profile is the one the
**marks** are in. A step that spends four sections on argument and one on a
calculation is discursive with a calculation in it.

## Quantitative

*Finance, accounting, statistics, economics with maths, engineering.*
**This is the only profile with courses actually built against it** — Corporate
Finance, Treasury Management and the economics steps. Everything in `RULES.md`
that mentions a figure was written from here, so this profile is the one that
needs the least translation and is also the one whose assumptions are invisible.

| Rule | How it reads here |
|---|---|
| **C-3** | Binding. Every method gets a worked example with the arithmetic visible. |
| **C-9** | Binding, and this is its home. Worked example, then an unworked one, marked by a numeric `check`. |
| **C-5** | A concrete anchor is a **figure attached to a decision**. A number alone is not enough — `2/10` contains a digit and taught nobody anything. |
| **E-1, E-4, E-5, E-6** | Binding as written. Workings line up, negatives in brackets, ZMW with the unit in the header, subtotals ruled off. |
| **E-2** | Binding. Every formula names its symbols. |
| **C-4** | Keep the course's own notation, even where a clearer one exists. The marker is holding the slide. |

**What goes wrong here:** the step becomes a solved-problems sheet. Every
section is a formula, a table and an answer, nothing is at stake, and **W-6**
and **W-14** quietly stop being applied because the arithmetic feels like
content. The check: cover every table in the step. Is there anything left?

## Rule-application

*Law, tax, audit, ethics, regulation, and the compliance halves of business
courses.*
**No course of this kind has been built yet.** What follows is derived from what
these papers ask for, and the first course written against it should correct it.

The reader is not asked to remember the rule. They are asked to take facts they
have never seen and say what the rule does to them — which is a **procedure**,
and it is the thing that is examinable.

| Rule | How it reads here |
|---|---|
| **C-3** | Read as: where the material works a **case**, the step works one too, with the reasoning visible line by line. The "arithmetic" is the chain from rule to facts to conclusion, and skipping a link is the same defect as skipping a line of a calculation. |
| **C-9** | **Binding, and it is the whole game.** The worked one applies the test to facts and reaches a conclusion. The unworked one gives a **different fact pattern** and stops. The `check`'s wrong options are the conclusions the common misreadings produce — the fact everyone fixes on that turns out not to matter, the exception missed. |
| **C-5** | A concrete anchor is a **named case, a section number, a set of facts, or a decided outcome**. A section that states a principle end to end is the same defect as a finance section with no figures. |
| **C-6** | Stronger than in finance. A leading case is the thing a student carries into the hall: name it, date it, say what the parties actually did, and say what was held. "X v Y is the authority" wastes it. |
| **E-1, E-4, E-5, E-6** | **n/a.** There is nothing to line up. Do not reach for a table because the finance steps have them. |
| **E-2** | **n/a**, unless the course genuinely has a formula. |
| **E-9 `cards`** | Strong fit. Two to four *kinds* — heads of a claim, categories of duty, the limbs of a test — is exactly the shape this profile produces, and it is the shape most often mistyped as a table. |
| **E-10 `kind: "warning"`** | Earns its place more than anywhere else: the distinction that looks obvious and is not is where the marks are lost. |
| **C-4** | Reads as: use the material's own **statutory language and case names**. Paraphrasing a statutory test into plainer words is the one place **W-16**'s "prefer the plain word" does **not** apply, because the exact words are the thing being applied. Explain them in plain words *around* the exact ones. |

**What goes wrong here:** the step teaches the rules and never applies one, so
the reader arrives at a fact pattern having memorised a list. If a step has no
facts in it, it has not started.

## Discursive

*History, sociology, politics, development, the strategy and theory halves of
business courses.*
**Partly exercised** — Strategic Management's seven steps sit here — and not yet
tested against a course that is discursive end to end.

The reader is asked to take a position and hold it up. There is rarely one right
answer, which is exactly why the rules that assume one need care.

| Rule | How it reads here |
|---|---|
| **C-3** | Read as: where the material works through an **argument**, the step works one too, showing why the evidence supports the claim rather than asserting that it does. |
| **C-9** | Read carefully. The worked one is a position argued with its evidence. The unworked one is **the counter-position**, handed to the reader: here is the other side, what is the strongest case for it? **The `check` cannot mark this**, and pretending otherwise is worse than not asking — so the check tests something with an answer (which theory predicts what, whose evidence supports which claim) and the open question sits in an `example` callout, unmarked and honest about being unmarked. |
| **C-5** | A concrete anchor is a **date, a place, a named figure, a real policy and what it did**. This profile is the one where a whole section can be abstract without anybody noticing, because abstraction is the register. |
| **C-6** | Binding. The case, event or study is what survives the exam. |
| **E-1, E-2, E-4, E-5, E-6** | **n/a** unless the course carries real data. |
| **E-9 `cards`** | Strong fit for schools of thought, positions, waves. |
| **S-5** | Hardest here. A wrong option must be a **position a thinking student actually holds**, not a straw man. If you cannot write three of those, the question has no answer and should not be a check. |

**What goes wrong here:** every side is given equally, nothing is at stake, and
the step reads as a summary of what people have said. **W-6** wants a claim
someone disagrees with, in the first two sentences.

## Procedural

*Practical accounting, IT and systems, lab and method courses, anything marked
on doing it in the right order.*
**No course of this kind has been built yet.**

| Rule | How it reads here |
|---|---|
| **C-3** | Binding, read as a full run-through of the procedure with every step visible, including the ones that look trivial. |
| **C-9** | Binding. The unworked one is the same procedure on different inputs, and the `check` marks the **output**, or the step where it goes wrong. |
| **C-5** | A concrete anchor is a real input, a real screen, a real output. |
| **E-1, E-6** | Binding wherever the procedure produces a working. |
| **`ul` vs `ol`** | ⚠️ **A gap in the block vocabulary.** The reader has `ul` only, and a procedure is ordered — **E**'s table says `ul` is for "genuinely unordered, parallel items", which a sequence is not. Until an `ol` exists, number the steps in the text of each item and **say so in the header comment**. A second procedural course should be the trigger to add the block rather than to keep working around it. |
| **E-10 `kind: "warning"`** | The step everyone gets wrong belongs in one. |

**What goes wrong here:** the step describes the procedure instead of running
it, so the reader can recite the order and has never seen the screen.

## Adding a profile

**Only after a course of that kind has been built**, never in advance. Each
section above should be a record of what a real course needed, and the two with
no course behind them say so at the top for exactly that reason — a reader
should know which of these is evidence and which is a considered guess.

When you add one:
1. Write it from the **past papers**, not from the subject's reputation.
2. Say what the exam asks the reader to **produce**, in one line.
3. Fill the table only for the rules that genuinely read differently. A profile
   that restates all of `RULES.md` is noise, and the next writer will skim it.
4. Name the course it came from. Naming a course as **evidence** is required;
   naming one in an **instruction** is what couples the skill to it.
